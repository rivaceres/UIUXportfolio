Agrilakal –
from the farm, straight to the buyer

A farmer-first marketplace. It connects Philippine growers, buyers, distributors and the government, from harvest listing to verified delivery and payment. The name joins agrikultura (farming) and kalakal (goods, trade).

Platform
Mobile, Tablet, Desktop
Service
UI/UX Design
Tools

Timeline
1 Week

Problem Statement

Filipino smallholder farmers often can’t find reliable buyers before harvest. Produce goes unsold or sells at low farmgate prices. Institutional buyers struggle to find supply and coordinate purchases across locations. Price information is uneven, logistics are fragmented, and there is no trusted record of what was agreed, delivered or paid.

Target Users

Primary Users

Farmers

Want to get verified, list harvests before picking, compare offers and get paid. They need simple step-by-step flows in their own language, low-bandwidth support, and LGU help when needed.

Buyers and wholesalers

Want to find crops by region, volume and date, and to negotiate and manage deliveries. They need verified sellers, a clear record of agreed terms, and a way to raise quality claims.

Distributors

Want predictable jobs within their area and capacity, with clear pickup details and a way to report breakdowns.

Secondary Users

DA National and Regional Administrators

Handle verification, disputes and reference prices.

LGU Agriculture Officers

Run local identity checks and assisted onboarding.

Goal

Farmers

Sell more of each harvest, earlier, at prices aligned to the regional reference, and know when they’ve been paid.

Buyers

Find verified supply fast, lock terms in one structured offer, and receive what they ordered on time.

Distributors

Get jobs that fit their area and capacity, with evidence that settles every handover.

DA and LGU

Onboard and assist farmers safely, with every sensitive action scoped and auditable.

Impact

This project produced a complete design package for Agrilakal: an information architecture, a user flow, wireframes, a design guide, a log of key design decisions, and high-fidelity screens for every role. The impact is still expected rather than measured.

I see Agrilakal working best as a government-backed platform, run with the Department of Agriculture and LGUs. It could give smallholders a fairer route to market, publish reference prices people can trust, and cut unsold and wasted harvests. It would also give officials live data on supply, prices and disputes, so they can plan programs and respond faster. To show whether it works, I would track sell-through rate as the north star, alongside time to first qualified offer, on-time fulfilment, payment completion and dispute incidence, with targets set after a pilot baseline.

Research & Key Insights

I didn’t run primary user research for this project. I based the problem, users and flows on secondary research: published reports and articles on Philippine smallholder farming, farmgate pricing, post-harvest loss and agricultural logistics.

  • Unsold harvests. Supply becomes visible to buyers too late, often only at or after harvest.
  • Low farmgate prices. Farmers have weak negotiating power and few market channels.
  • Information asymmetry. There is no shared, approved price reference, so middlemen hold the market information.
  • Fragmented logistics. Transport is arranged ad hoc, and delivery status relies on trust, not evidence.
  • Hard-to-find supply. Buyers can’t reliably locate volume across provinces.

Process

Ideation

The project began with establishing a clear information architecture to define the structure and flow of content across the platform. This helped outline key sections and ensure intuitive navigation.

Every role enters through the same onboarding and shares global elements: search, language, notifications and feedback states. After sign-in, each role lands on its own home, with navigation built around its main job.

Next, I mapped the end-to-end user flow, from a farmer’s harvest to a completed order, including key decision points involving farmers, buyers, the system, and distributors. This helped establish two important rules: an order is only considered complete once both delivery and payment are confirmed, and distributors cannot decline assigned jobs. Instead, they report any issues or exceptions so dispatch can reassign the job.

I then translated the key user journeys into low-fidelity wireframes to explore the initial layout, content structure, and navigation.

Final Designs

I designed 96 screens across four roles and three breakpoints, each shaped around one main job. Farmers can list a harvest, answer an offer and see when they’ll be paid. Buyers can turn a need into a purchase request, accept an offer and record payment. Distributors get jobs automatically, prove every pickup and drop-off, and can report a problem without being penalised. DA and LGU officials can see prices, volumes and disputes across their area.

Next Steps

Every decision in Agrilakal is still a hypothesis until real users test it, so my next steps would replace assumptions with evidence. First, I'd interview farmers, buyers, distributors and DA/LGU staff in Eastern Visayas to check the problem and roles I drew from secondary research. Then I'd run usability tests of the prototype on low-end Android phones with weak signal. Alongside that, I'd have native speakers review the Waray and Cebuano copy, and test contrast, text size and the icon-plus-label patterns with farmers who have low literacy or poor eyesight. From there, I'd co-design with the DA and LGUs on reference prices, farmer verification and links to existing registries like RSBSA. I'd also finish the missing states, such as offline sync, data-saver mode and SMS fallbacks, and align data handling with the Data Privacy Act of 2012. Finally, I'd pilot the platform in one province and measure the success metrics before scaling.
